What this is

A decision-teaching diagram, not a technique model. Each stroke is authored as a table of coaching phases — where the hands are, where the racquet points, how far the shoulders have turned ahead of the hips — and the elbows, knees, racquet tip and body position are solved from anatomy rather than drawn. The result is baked to joint coordinates at 60 fps and projected to 2D in the page itself, which is why the camera orbits and why all eight strokes cost 94 KB.

The ball is a consequence of the swing, not a second animation: its direction and speed come from how the string bed was moving at impact. Swing across the ball and it leaves across.

It exists to make a decision legible — where the ball is met, how far the shoulders lead the hips, what the swing path looks like from above. It is not a stroke-correction tool and should not be read as one.

What it does not model

Stated plainly, because these are the first things a coach will look for and none of them are present:

  • Grip and racquet face. No grip is modelled, and the string-bed angle is not represented at all. The racquet is a rigid line with a ring on the end.
  • Wrist and forearm. No wrist joint and no pronation. In a real serve and forehand these produce a large share of racquet-head speed.
  • Feet and weight transfer. Ankles are points. There is no foot orientation, no heel-to-toe roll, no ground reaction force.
  • Spine and shoulder girdle. The torso is one rigid segment with a lean. Real trunk motion is segmented, and the scapula moves independently.
  • Where the ball finishes. The ball now leaves along the swing — launch direction and speed are derived from the string bed's velocity, with quadratic drag and a topspin term. What is not calibrated is the landing position, so no in/out verdict is shown: the aim direction is authored, and the volley and return move the hand too little through contact to produce a realistic ball speed.
  • Individual variation. One generic right-handed body. No archetypes, no player styles, no grip-dependent contact height.

What is measured, and against what

Every figure on the readouts is computed from the animation, not typed in, and checked against a target band on each build. Racquet-tip speed at impact, forward-swing duration and shoulder–hip separation come from published biomechanics; knee flexion from a stance-comparison study. The contact-point geometry is coaching consensus, not measurement — the literature reports scalars, not 3D contact coordinates, so those bands are my construction and are labelled provisional in the build report.

Known to be wrong

Serve and overhead show a low swing-to-ball agreement (cos 0.35 and 0.23) because their racquet rotates hard through impact and this model has no pronation to carry that into the ball. The volley and return produce only 8 and 14 km/h, far short of real. The slice reaches 97° of knee flexion at contact against a 70–86° band. The two-hander meets the ball 8 cm off the body's centre line, which is shallow even for a stroke hit close in. Serve and overhead are struck 0.17 m and 0.12 m in front of the hips, which is less far into the court than a good server gets. These are open, not hidden.
