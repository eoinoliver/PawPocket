How to read this

Eight of these are legacy studies built from authored coaching phases — where the hands are, where the racquet points, how far the shoulders lead the hips — with the elbows, knees and racquet solved from anatomy. The ninth, the Djokovic forehand, is a source-plane study from ten hand-read poses off a single clip, and it is the only one with a retimed swing; it carries no ball flight because its flight was never validated.

None of the nine is coaching-approved, and none is motion capture. They are diagrams that are internally consistent and physically possible — two claims, not three.

What was just fixed

Hard failures across the eight legacy strokes went from ten to four. The serve and overhead were being levitated five metres into the air by a bad root offset — the serve's contact height read 6.22 m and every joint including the head hit 92 m/s. Both now contact at 2.56 m and 2.46 m, inside their literature bands. Four strokes had a planted foot sliding up to 0.64 m/s because the foot-pinning rule released it before it actually left the court.

What is still wrong

The elbow. On the one-hander, two-hander and serve an elbow exceeds the 16 m/s body cap. The cause is known exactly: the arm's inverse kinematics places the elbow along a fixed pole's component perpendicular to the arm, and when pole and arm line up that direction is ill-conditioned — on the serve the angle falls to 3.5° and the elbow hits 25 m/s while the authored wrist moves 5.5. It needs a pole field that can't line up with the arm, which is a design decision rather than a tweak, so it has been left visible rather than patched.

Four balls go the wrong way. The slice, serve, volley and return send the ball into the near half of the court. For the volley and return there's a prior cause: their racquet tip is doing 2.0 and 2.8 m/s at contact, so the hand barely moves through the ball and no sensible outgoing direction exists to compute. The swing has to be fixed before the direction.
